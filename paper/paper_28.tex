\begin{abstract}

Construction cost estimation relies on parametric catalogs in which tens of thousands
of items are generated from a small number of templates and differ only in attribute
values such as diameter, terrain type or work-shift conditions. Retrieving the exact
item, and not merely its family, is what determines whether a unit price is correct.
We benchmark lexical, neural, hybrid and structured retrieval on the 47,513 items of
one subcategory of the Spanish railway infrastructure catalog (ADIF), evaluating every
system at two levels: the exact parametric variant and its parent family.

The central finding is a systematic dissociation between those two levels.
Single-vector and learned-sparse multilingual retrievers place the correct family
first for 96.0--99.8% of queries while placing the correct variant first for only
7.0--13.6%; for GTE-multilingual-base the gap reaches 92.9 percentage points. The best
neural system, BGE-M3 with late interaction, reaches 44.8% at item level against 99.7%
at parent level. A tuned BM25 with parameter tokens shows almost no
such gap (97.4% item, 98.5% parent) and outperforms it by 52.6 points, while a
rule-based pipeline over extracted structured attributes reaches 90.3%. We also
quantify what makes the benchmark tractable for literal matching: the target contains
94.1% of the query's tokens and 99.96% of its numeric values verbatim.

Dense embeddings therefore encode the domain well enough to localise a query within
the catalog, but not the parametric distinctions that carry cost implications. The
dataset, the exact query set, per-query outputs and all configurations are released.

\end{abstract}

\section*{Glossary}

\begin{description}

    \item[Acc@1] Accuracy at rank 1 --- the proportion of queries for which the correct item appears in the top position.
 
    \item[ADIF] Spanish acronym for \textit{Administrador de Infraestructuras Ferroviarias}, the public entity responsible for railway infrastructure in Spain.

    \item[BC3 Format] Base de Costes de la Construcción, version 3 --- the standard data exchange format for Spanish construction cost databases, encoding hierarchical cost structures with parametric templates and work item descriptions.

    \item[BM25] A probabilistic ranking function that scores document relevance based on term frequency and document length normalization.

    \item[Dense Retrieval] A neural retrieval approach that represents queries and documents as dense embeddings, enabling semantic similarity search via vector spaces.

    \item[E5 Model] A sentence-transformer-based embedding model trained for general-purpose retrieval and semantic similarity tasks.
    
    \item[Hard-Negative Retrieval] A retrieval scenario where the correct item must be distinguished from highly similar candidates differing only in one or more critical attributes, such as a numeric specification.

    \item[Hybrid Retrieval] A method that combines lexical and neural approaches, often through score fusion or reranking, to balance precision and semantic coverage.

    \item[Item-Level / Parent-Level Evaluation] A dual evaluation protocol assessing retrieval at two granularities: item-level requires retrieving the exact parametric variant, while parent-level credits any item from the same parametric family.
    
    \item[Language Model (LM)] A lexical retrieval method that estimates the probability of generating a query from a document's language distribution.
    
    \item[MRR] Mean Reciprocal Rank — the average reciprocal of the rank position of the first relevant result across all queries.
    
    \item[nDCG] Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain --- a ranking metric that accounts for graded relevance and result position.

    \item[Template] The parametric definition of a work item: a description with named
    attributes (terrain type, diameter, work shift) whose values are left open. The OEB
    chapter contains 30 templates.

    \item[Variant (item)] One instance of a template, with every attribute bound to a
    concrete value. Variants are the retrieval targets; the catalog holds 47,513 of them.

    \item[Parent] The grouping that collects all variants of one template, identified by a
    shared six-character code. 	extit{Parametric family} is used interchangeably with
    	extit{parent}. Note that 	extit{model family} and 	extit{method family} refer to a
    different axis --- groupings of retrieval approaches --- and never to the catalog.

    \item[Parametric Catalog] A structured database where construction items are described by sets of parameters.  Each unique combination of parameter values generates a distinct catalog item.
    
    \item[Pseudo-Relevance Feedback (PRF)] A technique that expands the query using top-ranked documents from an initial retrieval round to improve recall.

\end{description}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
In modern construction project management, accurately retrieving the correct work item from standardized catalogs  is critical for budget control, procurement and stakeholder communication ~\cite{martinez-rojasApproachAutomaticClassification2015, natural_wu_2022}. Large infrastructure projects increasingly rely on parametric construction catalogs containing tens of thousands of highly similar items, where small lexical or numeric differences correspond to distinct technical specifications and unit costs. In this context, catalog retrieval is a precision-critical task rather than a general semantic search problem.

The challenge is compounded by terminology variability across stakeholders. Field workers, designers, and cost estimators often use different terms for identical concepts, creating a vocabulary mismatch between queries and catalog entries  ~\cite{chungComparingNaturalLanguage2023, role_martnezrojas_2016, rampiniArtificialIntelligenceConstruction2022, casiniAdvancedSiteManagement2022}. Moreover, construction descriptions frequently contain critical numeric parameters—dimensions, quantities, specifications—where even minor discrepancies can lead to costly errors in project estimation ~\cite{natural_wu_2022}. These domain-specific characteristics demand retrieval methods that balance lexical precision with semantic understanding while maintaining sensitivity to numeric values.

These terminology mismatches are particularly evident in two operational contexts. First, during cost estimation for design-bid-build tenders, estimators must map project specifications—often drafted with company-specific or region-specific wording—to standardized catalog entries. A project item description related to pipes must be matched to the precise catalog variant that accounts for terrain type, pipe diameter, and work-shift conditions, yet the source document may omit or paraphrase critical parameters. Second, during project execution, field engineers produce daily progress reports using abbreviated or colloquial item descriptions that must be reconciled with contractual line items for billing and compliance. A site note should map unambiguously to a specific catalog entry, but the terse phrasing and implicit context challenge both manual lookup and automated systems. In both scenarios, failure to retrieve the correct item carries tangible consequences: cost overruns from misquoted unit prices, contractual disputes over scope interpretation, or delays in payment certification. Robust retrieval methods that tolerate terminological variation while preserving numeric and parametric precision are therefore essential to practical construction workflows.

Both scenarios require two distinct capabilities, and this study is designed to separate them. The first is bridging vocabulary: recognising that an independently worded description refers to a given family of catalog concepts at all. The second is discriminating within that family: choosing, among hundreds or thousands of variants that differ only in attribute values, the one whose unit price applies. These are usually entangled, and a single accuracy figure cannot say which of them a system failed at.

We therefore hold the first fixed and vary only the second. Queries are the catalog's own short-form descriptions, so the vocabulary gap is deliberately small --- Section~\ref{subsec:overlap} measures exactly how small --- and every system is scored twice, once on the parametric family and once on the exact variant. The design gives lexical matching an easy ride by construction, and that is the point: with the vocabulary variable removed, whatever difficulty remains is parametric. What the results show is that this residual difficulty is severe, that it is not confined to any one architecture, and that a benchmark varying both capabilities at once would have hidden it.

Attempts to automate this mapping process predominantly employed rule-based systems and traditional machine learning methods, which often rely on structured data sets and expert-driven input ~\cite{caldasAutomatingHierarchicalDocument2003, natural_wu_2022}. Although these methods have shown limited success, they struggle with the domain-specific variability, nuanced language, and unstructured data prevalent in construction management ~\cite{rampiniArtificialIntelligenceConstruction2022, baekCriticalReviewTextbased2021}. Advances in natural language processing (NLP), particularly transformer-based models such as BERT, have shown promise in addressing these challenges by capturing semantic variability and contextual relationships ~\cite{tangAutomatedConstructionQuantity2022, dezaMachineLearningApproach2022}. However, issues such as data sparsity, domain-specific jargon, and computational resource demands persist ~\cite{chungComparingNaturalLanguage2023, martinez-rojasUsingClassificationTechniques2018}.

This study addresses one specific and well-defined part of that challenge through a systematic comparative evaluation of lexical, neural and hybrid retrieval methods on a large-scale parametric catalog from the railway infrastructure domain. The task is to retrieve the exact catalog item corresponding to a short description, among $47{,}513$ candidates that belong to a single subcategory and are generated from only $25$ parametric templates. Entries within a template therefore share almost all of their vocabulary and differ only in attribute values such as terrain type, pipe diameter or work-shift conditions. Queries are the catalog's own short-form descriptions rather than text written independently by practitioners; Section~\ref{subsec:overlap} quantifies exactly how much of each query is contained verbatim in its target.

Our contributions are the following. The primary contribution is an empirical characterisation of an existing failure mode rather than a new retrieval method.

\begin{itemize}
    \item We document a systematic dissociation between family-level and variant-level retrieval in parametric catalogs. Every multilingual dense retriever we evaluate identifies the correct parametric family almost perfectly and the correct variant almost never, with gaps of up to 92.9 percentage points. The dual evaluation protocol that exposes this --- scoring each system at both levels of the catalog hierarchy --- is itself transferable to any hierarchically organised technical catalog.
    \item We quantify the property of the benchmark that governs which methods succeed: how much of each query is already present verbatim in its target, lexically and numerically. Making this explicit is what distinguishes a genuine retrieval result from an artefact of how the evaluation pairs were constructed.
    \item We compare four families of approach under identical conditions --- lexical, neural, hybrid with reranking, and structured attribute matching over automatically extracted parameters --- and find that textual retrieval outperforms structured matching even when the latter is handed the correct template for free.
    \item We release a benchmark derived from the OEB subcategory of ADIF's parametric railway catalog: 47,513 items across 30 catalog concepts, of which 25 are parametric templates that generate 47,508 variants; the exact query set as a versioned list of identifiers, per-query outputs for every system, and the parsing tools that convert the raw BC3 format into a standard retrieval collection~\cite{bc3cat_dataset}.
\end{itemize}

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews prior work on construction classification and retrieval methods. Section 3 describes the ADIF dataset, the preprocessing pipeline, the query--target overlap that characterises the benchmark, and the retrieval configurations evaluated. Section 4 presents the results, organised around the item-to-parent dissociation. Section 5 discusses what the dissociation implies for deploying catalog search, and Section 6 concludes with directions for future research.

\section{Prior Work}

\subsection{Construction Classification Systems and Standards}

Construction work breakdown structures present a fundamental taxonomic challenge that distinguishes this domain from general information retrieval applications. National and organizational classification systems—including MasterFormat, UniFormat, OmniClass, and infrastructure-specific frameworks such as the ADIF (Spanish National Railway Manager) railway catalog \href{https://bpa.adif.es/bp1/#/welcome}{ADIF´s BPA Catalog} used in this study—impose hierarchical structures where items are organized by trade, material, location, or function ~\cite{royanoAnalysisClassificationSystems2023a, guvenConstructionClassificationSystem2022}. These parametric catalogs contain thousands to tens of thousands of work items characterized by structured attributes (dimensions, materials, performance specifications, unit costs) alongside free-text descriptions that exhibit substantial terminological variability. The ADIF catalog examined here, for instance, contains approximately 47,500 distinct work items spanning civil works, track infrastructure, electrification, and signaling systems.

The vocabulary mismatch problem is particularly acute in construction contexts. Practitioners use regional terminology, abbreviations, brand names, and informal descriptors that diverge significantly from standardized catalog entries. A single concept—such as a reinforced concrete beam of specific dimensions—may appear under dozens of lexical variants across project documents, specifications, and bills of quantities. This heterogeneity is further compounded in multilingual settings and when integrating catalogs from different organizations. 

Prior work has addressed this complexity through ontology-based approaches. The Industry Foundation Classes (IFC) and International Framework for Dictionaries (IFD) provide semantic frameworks for mapping between classification systems and enabling interoperability across software platforms. Wu et al.~\cite{wuNaturallanguagebasedIntelligentRetrieval2019} developed natural-language retrieval engines for BIM object databases using vector-space models enhanced with domain ontologies, establishing a paradigm where lexical retrieval is augmented with explicit semantic expansion. Liu et al.~\cite{43f8dc68b3d05e73df8185c5a051e207a64e4ed4} proposed Enhanced Explicit Semantic Analysis for construction product model retrieval, leveraging concept spaces derived from construction knowledge bases to bridge vocabulary gaps. Wang et al.~\cite{wangMultiscaleInformationRetrieval2021} extended this work with multi-scale BIM information retrieval using hierarchical structure modeling based on IFC. These foundational approaches established that effective retrieval in construction requires explicit handling of domain semantics, though they predate the neural revolution in information retrieval and have not been systematically compared against modern dense retrieval methods.

\subsection{Automated Classification and Retrieval in Construction}

The application of natural language processing and machine learning to construction document processing has grown substantially over the past decade. Early work by ~\cite{caldasAutomatingHierarchicalDocument2003} applied text classification to organize construction project documents, while subsequent studies extended these methods to bills of quantities, specifications, and regulatory texts. Systematic reviews by ~\cite{wuNaturalLanguageProcessing2022} and ~\cite{chungComparingNaturalLanguage2023} document the evolution from rule-based and bag-of-words approaches through support vector machines to contemporary transformer-based methods.

Most published work in construction NLP addresses category-level classification rather than item-level retrieval. Tang et al.~\cite{tangAutomatedConstructionQuantity2022} applied deep learning to construction specification classification; ~\cite{martinez-rojasUsingClassificationTechniques2018} developed automated text classification pipelines for public procurement documents using support vector machines and later neural approaches. de Sousa et al.~\cite{ebef73f3fbc92afecc62385adfa62bfe418b6749} recently reviewed automation approaches for the budgeting phase, finding that text classification methods achieve reasonable accuracy for category assignment but struggle with fine-grained item matching.

Critically, classification accuracy degrades as taxonomic granularity increases. Caldas et al.~\cite{caldasAutomatingHierarchicalDocument2003} reported 95.88\% accuracy for top-level MasterFormat divisions but substantially lower performance for fine-grained subcategories. Similarly, ~\cite{martinez-rojasApproachAutomaticClassification2015} achieved 92.4\% accuracy at the chapter level of work breakdown structures, with performance declining for detailed categorization. Deza et al.~\cite{dezaMachineLearningApproach2022} explored bidirectional LSTMs and Temporal Convolutional Networks for short construction text classification, demonstrating that even modern sequence models struggle with the brevity and density of catalog-style descriptions. These findings underscore that the granularity required for item-level retrieval—distinguishing among tens of thousands of near-identical entries—exceeds the capabilities demonstrated in existing classification studies.

\subsection{Technical Catalog Retrieval: Methods and Cross-Domain Evidence}

Information retrieval for technical catalogs has received extensive attention in e-commerce and manufacturing domains, offering methodological insights transferable to construction applications. The fundamental architectural choice—lexical versus neural retrieval—has been systematically evaluated in product search contexts with findings directly relevant to parametric construction catalogs.

Lexical methods, exemplified by BM25 and its variants, remain remarkably competitive for technical domains. Mendes et al.~\cite{mendesComparativeStudyLexical2025} compared BM25 against semantic embedding methods on governmental product catalogs and electronic invoices, finding that lexical retrieval achieved faster identification of relevant products while semantic methods improved ranking within retrieved sets. This complementarity motivates hybrid architectures. Chang et al.~\cite{changExtremeMultilabelLearning2021} demonstrated that sparse TF-IDF representations with extreme multi-label classification (the PECOS framework) achieved superior recall and latency on catalogs exceeding 100 million items compared to dense neural embeddings trained on the same data. Ubrangala et al.~\cite{ubrangalaSearchingFastSlow2024} showed that character-level TF-IDF outperformed word-level variants for SKU search with abbreviation-heavy queries—a finding directly applicable to construction catalogs where alphanumeric codes and abbreviated specifications are prevalent. These results suggest that technical identifiers, part numbers, and precise parametric specifications benefit from exact matching that dense representations may inadvertently blur.

Neural approaches have nonetheless advanced substantially in product retrieval. Dual-encoder architectures trained on large-scale click logs demonstrate strong performance when vocabulary mismatch is substantial and paraphrase recognition is required. Nigam et al.~\cite{nigamSemanticProductSearch2019a} at Amazon introduced semantic product search using bi-encoder transformers, achieving significant improvements in recall over lexical baselines. Choi et al.~\cite{choiSemanticProductSearch2020} developed structured matching modules that encode multi-field product information—titles, descriptions, dimensions, metadata—into unified representations that respect the heterogeneous structure of catalog entries. The CHARM architecture ~\cite{freymuthHierarchicalMultifieldRepresentations2025} proposed cascading hierarchical attention to align query tokens with specific product fields. More recent work explores learned sparse retrieval, where neural networks generate interpretable term expansions that combine lexical precision with semantic generalization. Biswas et al.~\cite{biswasEfficientInterpretableInformation2024} showed that hybrid encoders jointly learning dense and sparse representations can match cross-encoder effectiveness while maintaining first-stage retrieval efficiency—a critical consideration for production systems with latency constraints.

Relevant cross-domain work also addresses procurement and product standardization. The TPDR system ~\cite{cunhaTPDRNovelTwoStep2023} developed for intermediary procurement—including construction-related purchases—employs a two-stage architecture: dual transformer encoders for initial retrieval followed by syntactic reranking. This hybrid approach achieved substantial gains over purely lexical or purely semantic baselines when matching noisy, abbreviated initial specifications to standardized product descriptions. Cross-lingual product matching systems using hierarchical metric learning ~\cite{kooDeepMetricLearning2023} further demonstrate how taxonomy-aware training objectives can distinguish exact duplicates from substitutes and unrelated items—a distinction critical for construction catalogs where material grades or dimensional variants carry significant cost implications.

\subsection{Research Gap and Contribution}

Despite the maturity of retrieval methods in adjacent domains, no systematic benchmark exists for evaluating lexical, neural, and hybrid approaches on construction parametric catalogs. The AEC-specific literature remains dominated by ontology-enhanced lexical methods developed before the transformer era, with limited adoption of the dense retrieval and learned sparse techniques that have transformed e-commerce search. Meanwhile, the product search literature, while methodologically sophisticated, lacks evaluation on the particular characteristics of construction work items: dense parametric specifications with numeric constraints, deep hierarchical classification structures, and domain-specific terminology in languages beyond English. This gap leaves practitioners without empirical guidance when selecting retrieval architectures for construction applications.

This study addresses that gap through a systematic comparison of retrieval methods on a large-scale Spanish railway construction catalog. We evaluate classical lexical baselines (BM25 with domain-specific preprocessing including Spanish stemming and stopword handling), neural dense retrievers (multilingual sentence transformers fine-tuned for semantic similarity), and hybrid configurations that combine lexical and neural signals. Our experimental design encompasses 100 retrieval configurations across realistic query scenarios reflecting practitioner search behavior. The findings provide empirical guidance for practitioners implementing catalog retrieval systems and contribute the first rigorous benchmark for information retrieval on parametric construction work items, demonstrating conditions under which carefully tuned lexical methods remain competitive with—or superior to—neural alternatives.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Dataset Characteristics and Processing}

\subsubsection{Dataset Scope and Selection}

The experimental dataset derives from ADIF's parametric price catalog for Spanish railway infrastructure. While the complete catalog spans ten technical chapters (Architecture, Civil Works, Control Systems, Energy, Environmental Management, Protection and Security, Safety and Health, Telecommunications, and Track), this study focuses on the OEB subcategory within the Civil Works chapter, which addresses platform system infrastructure such as trenches, conduits, and piping systems, providing a controlled yet demanding testbed for retrieval evaluation. Extending the benchmark to additional chapters remains a priority for future work, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

The OEB subcategory provides an ideal testbed for retrieval evaluation as it exhibits high parametric variability while maintaining manageable scope for comprehensive analysis. Through parametric expansion of base templates across combinations of pipe materials, diameters (110mm, 160mm, 200mm), quantities (2, 4, 6, 8, 12 tubes), terrain types (normal, rocky, under tracks, road crossing, platform, attached, ballast), and work shift specifications (daytime, nighttime, exceptional conditions), the subcategory generates 47513 distinct item variants ~\cite{bc3cat_dataset}.

Each of these item variants belongs to a hierarchical structure:  
- The \emph{item-level} contains the fully specified variant, including all parameters (e.g., “6 PVC tubes, 160mm, under tracks, nighttime”).  
- The \emph{parent-level} refers to the shared base template that groups multiple such variants under a common parametric definition.

\subsubsection{BC3 Format Processing}

ADIF distributes its catalog in BC3 format (Base de Costes de la Construcción), the standard exchange format established by FIEBDC, a Spanish industry consortium that maintains data interchange standards for construction cost databases. We developed a custom parsing pipeline to transform the BC3 specification into structured JSON suitable for retrieval experiments.

The parsing pipeline handles three key transformations:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Parametric Expansion}: The BC3 format encodes base items with parameter placeholders (e.g., \texttt{\textbackslash No\_PIPES\textbackslash}, \texttt{\textbackslash TERRAIN\_TYPE\textbackslash}). The parser extracts parameter definitions, generates all valid combinations, and instantiates complete item descriptions by substituting parameter values into templates.
    
    \item \textbf{Dual-Text Extraction}: Each item contains two text fields: \textit{short-format} (condensed summary with parametric codes) and \textit{long-format} (detailed technical specification). The parser extracts both fields while preserving their association through unique item identifiers.
    
    \item \textbf{Metadata Preservation}: The parser retains hierarchical information including chapter codes, base template identifiers (first 6 characters), and parametric family relationships to enable parent-level evaluation.
\end{enumerate}

The complete parsing pipeline implementation is available in our code repository ~\cite{bc3cat_software}.

\subsubsection{Text Preprocessing}

All text fields undergo standardized preprocessing before indexing and retrieval. The preprocessing pipeline applies the following transformations:

\paragraph{Normalization}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Lowercase conversion for case-insensitive matching
    \item Whitespace standardization (multiple spaces → single space)
    \item Punctuation preservation except for sentence-final periods
    \item Numeric normalization (e.g., ``1,256.84'' for ``1256,84'')
    \item Accented character retention for proper Spanish processing
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Tokenization}
Different retrieval methods receive appropriately tokenized input:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Word tokens}: Regex pattern \texttt{\textbackslash w+} for lexical methods, yielding sequences like \texttt{["conduit", "concrete-cased", "2", "pipes", "pvc", "110", "mm"]}
    \item \textbf{Character n-grams}: Overlapping sequences of length 3, 4, and 5 for character-based methods (e.g., ``pvc'' → \texttt{["pvc", "\#pv", "pvc", "vc\#"]})
    \item \textbf{Subword tokens}: Pretrained tokenizers for neural methods (WordPiece for BERT-based models, SentencePiece for others)
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Parameter-Based Phrase Identification}
We leverage the catalog's parametric structure to identify domain-specific multi-word expressions. From the BC3 parameter definitions, we extract 127 multi-word parameter values (e.g., terrain types, work conditions, installation contexts) that function as atomic units in the classification system.

The preprocessing pipeline evaluated two phrase handling strategies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Additive}: Phrases supplement unigrams (``road'', ``crossing'', ``road\_crossing'')
    \item \textbf{Replacement}: Phrases substitute component words (``road\_crossing'' only)
\end{itemize}

This domain-specific phrase handling enables retrieval methods to align with the catalog's inherent parametric vocabulary.

\paragraph{Numeric Feature Extraction}
The pipeline identifies and normalizes integer quantities, decimal measurements with units, numeric ranges, and percentages.

Extracted numeric features enable exact numeric matching as a complementary retrieval signal (Section~\ref{subsec:hybrid_fusion}).

\subsubsection{How Much Do Query and Target Share?}
\label{subsec:overlap}

\subsubsection{Lexical Methods: BM25 and TF-IDF}
\label{subsec:lexical_methods}

\paragraph{BM25 Implementation}

We implement BM25 directly over sparse term--document matrices rather than through an existing retrieval toolkit, so that tokenization and parameter-phrase handling can be controlled explicitly. Term counts are produced with the \texttt{CountVectorizer} of scikit-learn v1.2.2~\citep{pedregosa2011scikit} (accent stripping, lowercasing, token pattern \texttt{\textbackslash b\textbackslash w+\textbackslash b}), and BM25 weights are computed from them following the Okapi formulation~\citep{robertson2009probabilistic}, with Robertson--Sp\"arck Jones inverse document frequency
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{idf}(t) = \log\!\left(\frac{N - \mathrm{df}(t) + 0.5}{\mathrm{df}(t) + 0.5} + 1\right)
\end{equation}
and the usual term-frequency saturation controlled by $k_1$ and $b$. Scoring is a sparse dot product between the query indicator vector and the weighted document matrix. We evaluate three tokenization variants, each with its own parameter grid:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{BM25-unigram}: Standard word tokenization\\
    $k_1 \in \{0.6, 0.8, 1.0, 1.2, 1.4\}$, $b \in \{0.20, 0.35, 0.50, 0.65, 0.80\}$ (25 combinations)
    \item \textbf{BM25-unibigram}: Word unigrams + bigrams\\
    $k_1 \in \{0.8, 1.0, 1.2, 1.4, 1.6\}$, $b \in \{0.35, 0.50, 0.65, 0.80, 0.95\}$ (25 combinations)
    \item \textbf{BM25-unigram-params}: Word tokens + parameter tokens\\
    $k_1 \in \{0.6, 0.8, 1.0, 1.2\}$, $b \in \{0.35, 0.50, 0.65\}$ (12 combinations)
\end{itemize}

The third variant needs to be described precisely, because it is the strongest system in the study and it is not merely a tokenisation choice. Each item is indexed as its normalised text followed by one token per resolved parameter axis, minted from the axis label and the value: an item on soft terrain with pavement reinstatement and a relevant work volume contributes \texttt{param\_terreno\_blando}, \texttt{param\_pavimento\_con\_reposici\'on} and \texttt{param\_condiciones\_de\_ejecuci\'on\_volumen\_relevante}. These tokens do not occur in the text; they encode the item's position in its template's parameter space, and within a template that encoding is unique for every one of the $47{,}513$ items.

The same representation is applied to the query, whose tokens are read from its own catalog record. This is a property of the task rather than a concession: both sides of a catalog-to-catalog match are catalog records, and a record's parameter values are a declared field of the BC3 source, not something inferred from its prose. Using them is using the data the format provides. What is not obvious is how much they are worth, and whether the result depends on their being read rather than derived; Section~\ref{subsec:structured_results} measures both.

This yields 62 total configurations. Documents and queries undergo identical preprocessing before indexing. No stopword list is applied, because terms such as ``de'' or ``en'' participate in the parameter phrases that distinguish catalog variants.

All 62 configurations are scored on a validation set of $5{,}000$ queries that is disjoint from the test set, and the configuration reported for each family is the one that maximises item-level Acc@1 there. The validation queries are sampled (seed 20260909) from the $30{,}923$ catalog items that are never used as test queries, so the test set remains the same $16{,}590$ queries used by every other system in this study and no comparison in the paper is affected by the split. Section~\ref{subsec:lexical_results} reports what selecting on the test set instead would have been worth.

\paragraph{TF-IDF Implementation}
TF-IDF baselines use scikit-learn v1.2.2~\citep{pedregosa2011scikit} with the following configurations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{TF-IDF-unigram}: Word unigrams with standard stopword removal
    \item \textbf{TF-IDF-unigram-nonstop}: Word unigrams without stopword removal
    \item \textbf{TF-IDF-char}: Character n-grams ($n \in \{3,4,5\}$) with word boundary markers
    \item \textbf{TF-IDF-phrases-add}: Word unigrams augmented with parameter-derived phrase tokens (e.g., ``High volume'')
    \item \textbf{TF-IDF-phrases-replace}: Multi-word parameter phrases replaced by single tokens
\end{itemize}
All TF-IDF variants use cosine similarity for ranking with L2-normalized vectors.

\subsubsection{Pseudo-Relevance Feedback Methods}

We evaluate two PRF methods using the same parameter grid: feedback documents $M \in \{5, 10\}$, expansion terms $N \in \{20, 40\}$, and feedback weight $\beta \in \{0.5, 0.7\}$, yielding 8 configurations each (the two weakest β=0.7, N=40 variants are
omitted for brevity; full results in the repository).

Both are implemented directly over the BM25 retriever described above, and share one procedure: retrieve, take the top $M$ documents as the pseudo-relevant set, score the terms occurring in it, keep the top $N$, and re-query. The two variants differ only in how terms are scored --- RM3 weights them by their frequency in the feedback set against a collection-frequency background, Rocchio by normalised term frequency over the feedback set alone, with no negative feedback ($\gamma = 0$), which the single-relevant-item setting makes meaningless.

The expansion is applied at the level of the query string: each selected term is repeated a number of times proportional to its weight, and the repeated terms are appended to the original query, which is left intact. The parameter $\beta$ therefore scales the repetition budget rather than interpolating two score distributions, which is how it functions in the canonical formulations~\citep{abdul2004umass, rocchio1971relevance}. The mechanism is equivalent in effect --- more repetitions give the expansion terms more weight in the BM25 score --- but it is an approximation, and the results in Section~\ref{subsec:prf} should be read as such: they bound what term-based query expansion achieves on this benchmark rather than certifying a particular estimator.

\subsubsection{Neural Dense Retrieval Methods}
\label{subsec:neural_methods}

All neural models are used in zero-shot mode without fine-tuning on construction text, testing their transfer capabilities to domain-specific Spanish technical language.

\paragraph{E5 Model}
We evaluate \texttt{intfloat/multilingual-e5-base} (278M parameters, 768 dimensions) using the sentence-transformers library v2.2.2~\citep{reimers2019sentence}. Following the model documentation, queries receive the prefix ``query: '' and documents receive ``passage: '' before encoding. Embeddings are L2-normalized before similarity computation.

\paragraph{Spanish Sentence Similarity}
We evaluate \texttt{hiiamsid/sentence\_similarity\_spanish\_es}, a model specifically fine-tuned for Spanish semantic similarity tasks, to compare against multilingual alternatives.

\paragraph{GTE Model}
We evaluate \texttt{Alibaba-NLP/gte-multilingual-base} with two query formats:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{GTE-direct}: Standard ``query:'' prefix
    \item \textbf{GTE-instruct}: Spanish instruction prefix: ``query: Lookup for the most similar document to this query: [query]''
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{BGE-M3 Model}
BGE-M3 (\texttt{BAAI/bge-m3}, 568M parameters) supports three retrieval modes within a unified architecture~\citep{chen2024bge}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{BGE-M3-dense}: Single 1024-dimensional vector per text
    \item \textbf{BGE-M3-sparse}: Learned sparse weights (SPLADE-style)
    \item \textbf{BGE-M3-colbert}: Token-level embeddings with ColBERT-style late interaction
\end{itemize}
The ColBERT mode computes relevance as the sum of maximum token similarities. All BGE-M3 modes use the same encoder with mode-specific output layers.

\subsubsection{Hybrid Fusion and Reranking}
\label{subsec:hybrid_fusion}

The fusion process consisted of three sequential steps applied to each query independently.

First, retrieval scores from each ranker were normalized using min-max scaling to ensure comparability across heterogeneous scoring functions. For a given query $q$, document $d$ and ranker $r$, each document score $s_{d,r}$ was transformed as:

\begin{equation}
s'_{d,r} = \frac{s_{d,r} - \min_r(s)}{\max_r(s) - \min_r(s)}
\end{equation}

Second, we applied numeric parameter bonuses to account for matches between query parameters and document metadata. Each document received an additional weighted reward based on exact parameter matches ($\beta_{\text{exact}}$) and partial parameter overlaps ($\beta_{\text{overlap}}$). These bonuses were added to the normalized scores before fusion.

Third, the adjusted scores from all retrievers were combined using weighted linear combination (COMBSUM):

\begin{equation}
s_{\text{final}}(d) = \sum_{r \in R} w_r \cdot s'_{d,r}
\end{equation}

where $w_r$ represents the fusion weight for ranker $r$, and $\sum_{r} w_r = 1$.

To identify optimal fusion configurations, we conducted a systematic parameter sweep over the weight space for several hybrid strategies: (1) three-way fusions combining BM25 with one BGE-M3 variant and character-level TF-IDF, and (2) a five-way fusion incorporating BM25, all three BGE-M3 variants (dense, sparse, ColBERT), and TF-IDF. For each configuration, we evaluated combinations of $\beta_{\text{exact}} \in \{0.0, 0.05, 0.10, 0.15\}$ and $\beta_{\text{overlap}} \in \{0.0, 0.05\}$ across a grid of weight distributions. The optimal configuration for each strategy was selected on item-level Acc@1. Unlike the lexical grids, the fusion sweep is selected on the test set; the validation experiment of Section~\ref{subsec:lexical_results} bounds what that is worth for the lexical families, and the hybrid results should be read as an upper bound on what fusion achieves here rather than as an out-of-sample estimate.

\paragraph{Cross-Encoder Reranking}
We employ a two-stage cascade: efficient first-stage retrieval followed by expensive cross-encoder reranking of top candidates. Cross-encoder implementation uses:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Model: \texttt{cross-encoder/mmarco-mMiniLMv2-L12-H384-v1}, a multilingual reranker trained on mMARCO, the machine-translated multilingual version of MS MARCO, whose training languages include Spanish
    \item Input format: \texttt{[CLS] query [SEP] document [SEP]}, truncated to 320 tokens
    \item Reranking depth: top 20, 50 and 100 candidates from the first stage
    \item Base retrievers: BM25-unigram, BGE-M3-ColBERT, and the three-way hybrid
    \item Score combination: \textit{replace} (cross-encoder scores only) and \textit{blend} (interpolation with the first-stage score, $\lambda = 0.6$)
\end{itemize}

The cross-encoder produces a relevance score for each query--document pair through joint encoding and a classification head.

\subsubsection{Query Expansion with Generated Documents}
\label{subsec:hyde_method}

HyDE~\citep{gao2023precise} addresses the opposite problem to the one this benchmark poses: it asks a language model to write the document that would answer the query, and retrieves with that text instead, on the assumption that a generated document shares more vocabulary with real documents than a short query does. Here the query already contains most of its target's vocabulary (Section~\ref{subsec:overlap}), so the expected effect is negative. We evaluate it anyway, because a negative result with a measured mechanism says more about the benchmark than an omission would.

One hypothetical document is generated per query by Phi-4 (14B, greedy decoding, $110$ tokens) from the query alone, with no retrieved context. Three query formulations are compared on the same retrievers and the same queries: the original query, the hypothetical document alone, and the two concatenated. Because generation costs about a second per query, this experiment runs on a subsample of $1{,}976$ test queries stratified by template, and the baselines are recomputed on that subsample so that every comparison is paired.

\subsubsection{Structured Attribute Matching}
\label{subsec:structured}

Every system described so far treats the catalog as free text. A parametric catalog invites the opposite treatment: if an item is a template plus a tuple of attribute values, the natural tool is not ranking but extraction followed by an exact lookup. We implement that alternative as a baseline family, so that the comparison in Section~\ref{subsec:structured_results} is between methods and not between a method and an assumption.

The pipeline has three stages:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Template identification.} The query is encoded with multilingual-e5-base and matched against the item-level dense index; the template of the top-ranked item is taken as the query's template.
    \item \textbf{Parameter extraction.} Given that template, its schema --- the set of axes and the closed list of admissible values for each --- is used to assign a value to every axis from the query text. An axis that cannot be resolved is left unset.
    \item \textbf{Catalog lookup.} The extracted tuple is matched against the items of that template. A fully resolved tuple identifies exactly one item; an unset axis acts as a wildcard, so a partially resolved tuple identifies the subset consistent with whatever was resolved.
\end{enumerate}

The output is assembled into a ranking of $K$ items in three tiers: the items returned by the lookup, then the remaining items of the same template, then the dense ranking of stage~1 as filler. Within a tier the order is the catalog's own, because the lookup returns set membership and not a graded score. That property is not incidental --- Section~\ref{subsec:structured_results} shows it governs how these systems fail.

Stages 1 and 3 are held fixed across configurations, which differ only in how stage 2 assigns values:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rule-based:} normalised string matching of each admissible value against the query, plus a small set of regular expressions for numeric axes written in running text (\textit{``de 2 tubos''}, \textit{``3 T,''}).
    \item \textbf{Phi-4 classification:} the admissible values of each axis are presented to Phi-4 (14B, served through Ollama) as a closed set, with the instruction to copy one of them verbatim or answer \texttt{null}.
    \item \textbf{Llama-3.1-8B extraction:} the same schema is shown, but the model is asked to extract the value of each axis from the query rather than to select from the list, so its answers must afterwards be normalised back onto the admissible set.
    \item \textbf{Fine-tuned classifier:} multilingual-e5-base with one linear classification head per (template, axis) pair --- 97 heads over the 25 parametric templates --- each predicting one admissible value or \texttt{null}.
    \item \textbf{Fine-tuned BIO tagger:} token-level sequence labelling over the same encoder, tagging value spans that are then normalised against the schema.
\end{itemize}

The two supervised extractors are trained on the long-form item description and applied to the short form that serves as query. This is deliberate: the query text is never seen in training, so their evaluation here is a cross-distribution test rather than a re-reading of the training distribution. Section~\ref{subsec:structured_results} reports what that costs.

Because stage 1 is a dense retriever, it is being asked for exactly what Section~\ref{sec:results} shows dense retrievers do well --- the family, not the variant. To separate its contribution from the rest, the three strongest configurations are also run with stage 1 replaced by the ground-truth template. Those variants do not grant perfect extraction; stage 2 is unchanged. They bound only what template identification costs.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}

\subsubsection{Retrieval Task Definition}

The evaluation task simulates realistic construction catalog search: given a short-form description (\textit{resumen}), retrieve the corresponding detailed specification (\textit{texto}) from 47,513 candidates. Each query has exactly one correct target at the item level, though multiple items may share the same parametric parent.

We construct the evaluation set by:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A random sample of 16,590 from the \textit{resumen} descriptions was selected as query inputs
    \item All 47,513 \textit{texto} descriptions were used as the retrieval corpus
    \item Ground truth mappings were defined using shared item identifiers
    \item The full catalog was retained as distractors-no filtering or candidate pruning was applied
\end{enumerate}

The sample size (16,590) ensures 99\% confidence with ±1\% margin of error:
\[
n = \frac{Z^2 \cdot p(1 - p)}{E^2} = \frac{(2.576)^2 \cdot 0.5 \cdot 0.5}{(0.01)^2} = 16{,}590
\]
where $n$ is the required sample size, $Z$ is the Z-score for the confidence level (2.576 for 99\%), $p = 0.5$ assumes maximum variance, and $E$ is the margin of error (0.01). This ensures statistically robust comparisons across methods.

\subsubsection{Dual-Target Evaluation}

We implement two complementary evaluation perspectives:

\paragraph{Item-Level (Exact Match)}
Requires retrieving the specific parametric variant. For query with ID ``OEB020aabdcfa'', only the document with identical ID counts as correct. This strict criterion applies to scenarios requiring precise specifications such as material procurement or detailed cost estimation.

\paragraph{Parent-Level (Family Match)}
Credits retrieval of any variant from the same parametric family, identified through shared 6-character prefixes. For query ``OEB020aabdcfa'', any document with ID starting ``OEB020'' counts as correct. This relaxed criterion acknowledges scenarios where parametric siblings are functionally equivalent, such as preliminary estimation or category identification.

The dual-target approach enables nuanced analysis: methods may excel at family identification while struggling with variant-level disambiguation.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{subsec:metrics}

We employ four complementary metrics to assess retrieval quality. Because each query has exactly one correct target, Accuracy@1 coincides with Precision@1 and MRR with Mean Average Precision, so the latter is not reported separately:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Accuracy@1 (Success@1)}: Proportion of queries where the correct item appears in rank 1. Critical for automated systems where only the top result is used.
    
    \item \textbf{Recall@k}: Proportion of queries with correct item in top-$k$ results. We report Recall@5 and Recall@10 as reasonable examination depths for human review.
    
    \item \textbf{Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR)}: Average of reciprocal ranks of first correct result.
    
    \item \textbf{Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (nDCG@10)}: Accounts for rank position using logarithmic discount
\end{enumerate}

Metrics are computed with a purpose-written evaluator rather than a general-purpose library, because the parent-level criterion requires a non-standard ideal ranking: any of the sibling variants under the correct parent counts as relevant, so the nDCG denominator must be normalized against the number of siblings present in the catalog rather than against a single relevant document. The evaluator is released with the code, and per-query outputs are provided so that any metric can be recomputed independently.

\subsubsection{Query Subset Analysis}

Performance analysis across query subsets reveals how methods handle different retrieval challenges. We partition the evaluation set into two categories:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{HAS\_NUM} (16,561 queries, 99.83\%): Queries containing at least one numeric parameter (e.g., ``2 tubos'', ``110 mm''). Tests whether methods can match quantitative specifications.

    \item \textbf{NO\_NUM} (29 queries, 0.17\%): Queries without numeric content, containing only textual descriptors. Isolates purely textual matching performance.

\end{itemize}

The split is extremely unbalanced because nearly every catalog description encodes at least one dimension or quantity. The NO\_NUM subset is therefore reported for completeness rather than as a basis for statistical comparison.

Subset analysis identifies method strengths (e.g., neural models may excel on NO\_NUM while lexical methods dominate HAS\_NUM).

\subsubsection{Statistical Significance Testing}

We employ paired bootstrap resampling to determine whether observed performance differences reflect genuine method advantages rather than random variation~\citep{smucker2007comparison}.

For each system pair $(A, B)$, we compute per-query metric differences, generate $B=10{,}000$ bootstrap samples with replacement, and estimate $p$-values from the empirical distribution of mean differences. We apply the Holm--Bonferroni step-down correction for multiple comparisons and report improvements as significant only when $p < 0.05$ after correction.

\subsubsection{Error Analysis}
\label{subsec:error_taxonomy}

To understand failure modes we classify \emph{every} failed query --- not a sample --- for three representative systems: BM25-unigram, BGE-M3-ColBERT and the three-way hybrid. All three are evaluated on the same 16,590 queries, so their failure sets are directly comparable. A rule-based classifier assigns each failure to exactly one of five categories:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Numeric Mismatch}: the retrieved item differs from the target in a quantitative parameter (e.g. ``110 mm'' vs ``160 mm'', ``2 tubos'' vs ``4 tubos'').

    \item \textbf{Near-Duplicate Error}: the retrieved item is a parametric sibling that differs only in non-numeric attributes.

    \item \textbf{Lexical Confusion}: high lexical overlap masks a critical distinction, particularly in work-condition modifiers (standard vs exceptional shifts) or scope descriptors (supply-only vs supply-and-installation).

    \item \textbf{Ranking Failure}: the correct item is retrieved but ranks beyond the examination depth considered (position 11 or later).

    \item \textbf{Other}: failures matching none of the above.
\end{enumerate}

Two of the five --- Near-Duplicate Error and Ranking Failure --- have zero frequency in every method, and are omitted from the results tables rather than reported as rows of zeros.

\subsection{Implementation Details}
\label{subsec:implementation}

All experiments run under Python 3.11.8 in a single container image. Lexical retrieval (BM25, TF-IDF) and pseudo-relevance feedback (RM3, Rocchio) are implemented directly over sparse matrices built with scikit-learn v1.2.2~\citep{pedregosa2011scikit} and SciPy; neural models are served through sentence-transformers v2.2.2~\citep{reimers2019sentence} with PyTorch 2.2.2, and BGE-M3 through FlagEmbedding. Retrieval metrics are computed by a purpose-written evaluator (Section~\ref{subsec:metrics}). The one exception is \texttt{gte-multilingual-base}, which cannot be loaded by this software stack and is evaluated separately; the reason and its consequences are documented in Section~\ref{subsec:gte}. Experiments run on a workstation with an Intel i9-13900KF CPU and an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU.

For reproducibility, the exact set of $16{,}590$ evaluation queries is released as a versioned list of catalog identifiers rather than regenerated by sampling, model versions are pinned, and complete configurations are stored in YAML files. Code, data, per-query outputs and configurations are openly available at~\cite{bc3cat_software,bc3cat_dataset}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
The results across all evaluated configurations converge on a consistent finding: lexical methods, when the query carries the item's parameter values as explicit tokens, outperform neural and hybrid approaches at the item level by a substantial margin. The following subsections detail the performance of each method family, culminating in a cross-method comparison in Section~\ref{sec:4.7_Overall Performance Summary}.

\subsection{Lexical Methods Comparative Analysis}

Both evaluations used the same test set of 16,590 queries.

At the parent level (Figure~\ref{fig:lexical_performance_parent}), TF-IDF methods dominated, with TF-IDF-unigram\_phrases\_replace achieving the highest Acc@1 (0.990). BM25 variants showed more modest parent-level performance, ranging from 0.873 to 0.985.

Attributing the whole $26.5$ to length normalisation, as one might from the figure alone, would conflate three effects that pull in different directions.

\paragraph{What the grid search was worth.}
Reporting the best cell of a grid that was scored on the test set makes the reported figure optimistic by an unknown amount. Table~\ref{tab:validation_selection} replaces the unknown with a measurement: the same 62 configurations were scored on the disjoint validation set of $5{,}000$ queries, the winning cell of each family was taken from there, and only then was it scored on the test set.

\input{tables/validation.tex}

For the two families that carry the paper's claims the two splits choose the same cell, so the optimism is exactly zero: BM25 with parameter tokens is $(k_1{=}0.60, b{=}0.35)$ under either criterion, and its $0.9737$ is an out-of-sample figure. For BM25-unigram the validation set prefers $(1.00, 0.35)$ where the test set prefers $(0.60, 0.35)$, a difference of $0.14$ points --- smaller than the gap between any two systems compared in this paper. The reported BM25-unigram runs keep $(0.80, 0.35)$, the configuration on which the hybrid fusions and the reranking cascades of Sections~\ref{subsec:hybrid_results} and~\ref{subsec:reranking} were built; on the test set it scores $0.8691$, between the two.

Two properties of the grid explain why selection turns out to be so cheap here. First, the two splits order the configurations almost identically: Spearman's $\rho$ between validation and test scores is $0.995$ for the uni+bigram grid, $0.993$ for the unigram grid and $0.972$ for the parameter-phrase grid. Where the two splits do disagree it is among cells that are already tied for practical purposes: the five best uni+bigram cells appear in the same order on both, and the three best parameter-phrase cells do, while the unigram grid --- whose top four cells span $0.17$ points on the test set --- is the one that reorders. Second, the validation split is uniformly easier --- every one of the 62 configurations scores higher on it than on the test set, by between $0.10$ and $1.16$ points --- so the offset shifts all cells together instead of reordering them. This is what makes the split usable for selection and unusable as an estimate of absolute performance, and it is why the test figures are reported separately rather than being read off the validation column.

Table~\ref{tab:statistical_significance} reports every pairwise comparison among the eight lexical configurations, without selection: $28$ pairs, all significant after correcting across the whole family. The narrowest gap in the table is $0.8$ points, between the two weakest TF-IDF variants, and it is the only comparison whose corrected $p$ exceeds $0.001$. With $16{,}590$ paired observations, differences of this size are resolvable; the substantive question is which of them matter, not which of them are detectable. BM25 with parameter tokens leads the next best configuration, BM25-unigram, by $10.5$ points, and the best TF-IDF variant by $26.5$; BM25-unigram in turn exceeds the TF-IDF family by $16.1$ to $31.1$ points and BM25-unibigram by $4.2$.

\input{tables/significance.tex}

Every lexical configuration is more accurate on the $29$ queries that contain no numeric value than on the $16{,}561$ that do, and for the five TF-IDF variants the difference is the difference between $1.000$ and $0.558$--$0.708$. The subset is too small to carry a claim on its own; it is consistent with the numeric mismatches that Section~\ref{subsec:error_analysis} finds to be almost the only failure mode of every system here. Per-subset metrics for all configurations are in the released artefacts.



Based on item-level results, BM25 with parameter tokens ($k_1=0.60$, $b=0.35$) is the best-performing lexical configuration, ahead of every other lexical variant on all evaluation metrics with statistical significance. It is used as the strong base for the pseudo-relevance feedback experiments of Section~\ref{subsec:prf}.

\subsection{Pseudo-Relevance Feedback Methods}
\label{subsec:prf}

Pseudo-relevance feedback (PRF) was evaluated to assess whether query expansion can improve on a lexical retriever in this domain. Two approaches were tested, RM3 and Rocchio, over two base retrievers: BM25-unigram ($k_1=0.80$, $b=0.35$; Acc@1 $=0.869$), and the best lexical configuration of Section~\ref{subsec:lexical_results}, BM25 with parameter tokens ($k_1=0.60$, $b=0.35$; Acc@1 $=0.974$). Expanding only the weaker base would leave open whether the null result is a property of PRF or of the headroom left by a mediocre first stage.

Over the strong base, neither method helps. The best RM3 configuration reaches $0.9732$ against a baseline of $0.9737$, a difference of $-0.04$ percentage points; the best Rocchio configuration reaches $0.9672$, a degradation of $0.64$ points. The result over the weak base, reported in detail below, is the same in direction: RM3 neutral, Rocchio harmful.

Table~\ref{tab:prf_comparison} reports the best RM3 and the best Rocchio configuration over each base, from grids of eight configurations each ($M \in \{5, 10\}$, $N \in \{20, 40\}$, two interpolation weights).

\input{tables/prf.tex}

The result is the same on both bases and in both directions. RM3 is neutral: it moves the weak base by $+0.08$ points and the strong one by $-0.05$, neither of them significant. Rocchio degrades both, by $1.3$ and $0.7$ points respectively. Within each grid the configurations are almost indistinguishable --- the six reported RM3 runs over the weak base span $0.8693$ to $0.8699$ --- with one exception worth noting: Rocchio loses about two points whenever $N=40$, which is the only place in these grids where a hyperparameter matters, and it matters by making things worse.

Reporting both bases is what makes the null result interpretable. Over a mediocre first stage one could argue that expansion had nothing to work with; over a first stage at $0.974$ one could argue there was no headroom. Getting the same answer over both leaves neither explanation standing. In a collection where the target already contains $94.1\%$ of the query's terms, adding more terms to the query cannot help, and adding the wrong ones hurts.

\subsection{Neural Dense Retrieval Methods}

This subsection presents the comparative performance of neural dense retrieval models across three model families: E5, GTE, and BGE-M3. All models were evaluated in zero-shot mode on the same test set of 16,590 queries at both item-level and parent-level retrieval tasks.

\subsubsection{E5 Model Family Performance}

Multilingual-e5-base retrieves the correct item first for $0.136$ of queries --- roughly one in seven --- and the correct family for $0.982$.

The two levels differ by a factor of $7.2$: from $13.6\%$ at item level to $98.2\%$ at parent level, a gap of $84.5$ percentage points. The embeddings capture enough of the domain to place a query under the right template, and almost none of what separates the variants within it.

Stratification by query type points to where the loss occurs: on the 29 queries with no numeric parameters, Acc@1 is $0.897$, against $0.135$ on the 16,561 numeric ones. The non-numeric subset is far too small to carry a statistical claim, but the direction is consistent across every neural family evaluated here.

\subsubsection{GTE Model Family Performance}
\label{subsec:gte}

Two GTE runs isolate the effect of query formulation: both use \texttt{Alibaba-NLP/gte-multilingual-base} with identical document encoding, and differ only in whether the query carries a natural-language instruction prefix. Their five metrics at both levels are in Table~\ref{tab:dissociation} and in the released artefacts.

The two formulations are indistinguishable: $0.070$ versus $0.073$ at item level and $0.998$ versus $0.997$ at parent level. Adding a natural-language instruction to the query neither helps nor hurts on this task, which is consistent with queries that consist almost entirely of technical terms and numeric values, where an instruction prefix adds no disambiguating signal.

The pair also exhibits the largest item-to-parent gap of any system evaluated here: GTE places the correct parametric family first for $99.8\%$ of queries while placing the correct variant first for only $7.0\%$. The model has learned the domain well enough to localise a query within the catalog almost perfectly, and is nonetheless unable to choose among the variants that template generates --- $144$ of them for the median template, $6{,}336$ for the largest.

The same model reaches $0.586$ on the $29$ queries that contain no numeric value, against $0.069$ on the $16{,}561$ that do. The subset is far too small ($0.17\%$ of the test set) to carry a quantitative claim, and is reported only because the direction is unambiguous and identical for every neural system evaluated here: what these models fail to represent is the numbers.

\paragraph{A note on configuration} These figures differ substantially from those we initially obtained. \texttt{gte-multilingual-base} requires \texttt{trust\_remote\_code}, which the sentence-transformers version used for the other models does not support; the fallback encoder applied mean pooling, whereas this model is trained for CLS pooling, and the E5-style \texttt{query:}/\texttt{passage:} prefixes were applied to a model never trained with them. Under that configuration the model retrieves the correct item for essentially no query at all. We therefore evaluate GTE with CLS pooling, without prefixes, and with a newer \texttt{transformers} release; all other models use the environment described in Section~\ref{subsec:implementation}. We report this explicitly because the failure was silent: the encoder fell back without raising an error, and the index metadata did not record which encoding path had been taken.

The three modes share one $568$M-parameter encoder and differ only in how its output is used. That makes the comparison unusually clean, and the result is the dissociation in its purest form. Single-vector compression, then, is what costs the variant, and it costs the same whether the vector is dense or sparse: pooling a document into one representation preserves what family it belongs to and discards which member of it. \subsubsection{Cross-Family Performance Comparisons}
\label{subsec:cross_family}

Table~\ref{tab:dissociation} places every method evaluated in this study side by side at both levels of the catalog hierarchy. It is the central result of this work.

\input{tables/item_parent_dissociation.tex}

Three observations follow. First, the ordering at item level is almost exactly reversed at parent level: the systems that discriminate variants worst are among those that identify families best. Second, the gap widens monotonically as item-level accuracy falls, from $1.2$ percentage points for BM25 with parameter tokens to $92.9$ for GTE. Third, the single exception --- \texttt{sentence\_similarity\_spanish\_es}, with a gap of only $37.1$ points --- is the exception that confirms the pattern: it is a monolingual sentence-similarity model rather than a retriever, and it is weak at both levels, so it never reaches the regime in which the dissociation appears.

The dissociation is not an artefact of any single architecture, and it is not confined to neural retrieval. It holds for single-vector dense encoders, for learned sparse representations and, attenuated, for late interaction --- but it also holds for the lexical family, which is where it is easiest to overlook because the item-level numbers there are high enough to look like success. TF-IDF with parameter phrases places the correct family first for $99.0\%$ of queries and the correct variant for $70.8\%$: a gap of $28.1$ points, larger than that of BGE-M3 ColBERT relative to its own item-level score. BM25-unigram gives up $10.4$ points the same way. Section~\ref{subsec:error_analysis} shows the phenomenon at the level of the representation itself: for $6.94\%$ of queries, BM25-unigram assigns the target and at least one sibling variant \emph{identical} vectors, so no ranking function operating on that representation can separate them.

What separates the methods, then, is not whether they understand the domain --- at parent level almost all of them are close to ceiling --- but how much of the parameter values their representation preserves. Dense encoders discard nearly all of it, lexical models most of the time keep enough, and the difference is one of degree.

BGE-M3-ColBERT is therefore the neural system carried forward into the fusion and reranking experiments: at $0.448$ it is $3.3$ times more accurate than multilingual-e5-base and $6.4$ times more accurate than GTE, and it is still wrong on more than half of the queries.

\subsection{Hybrid Fusion Results}
\label{subsec:hybrid_results}

Table~\ref{tab:hybrid_fusion} presents the best configuration found by the parameter sweep for each fusion strategy.

The numeric bonuses behave in a way worth reporting. Three of the four fusions select $\beta_{\text{exact}}=0$ --- that is, they discard the exact-containment bonus entirely --- and all four select $\beta_{\text{overlap}}=0.05$. The reason is visible in the candidate pool: exact containment holds for the correct document in $99.8\%$ of queries, but also for $32.1\%$ of the retrieved distractors. A binary bonus that fires for roughly a third of the candidate list raises them all equally and cannot separate the correct variant from its siblings. Partial numeric overlap, being graded, does discriminate, though its contribution is small: it improves Acc@1 by between $0.3$ and $1.9$ percentage points depending on the fusion.

This is a negative result about a design element we expected to help. In a catalog where almost every target already contains all of the query's numeric values, the presence of those values carries little information; what would carry information is their \emph{absence} from the competing variants, which a containment bonus does not measure.

What the sweep does with the weights is more informative than the scores it reaches. \subsection{Cross-Encoder Reranking}
\label{subsec:reranking}

Cross-encoder reranking was applied to the top $K'$ candidates of three first-stage systems --- BM25 (unigram), BGE-M3-ColBERT, and the three-way hybrid --- at three depths, $K' \in \{20, 50, 100\}$. Two strategies were evaluated: \textit{replace}, which ranks by cross-encoder score alone, and \textit{blend}, which interpolates the cross-encoder score with the first-stage score ($\lambda = 0.6$).

Table~\ref{tab:reranking_results} presents all eighteen combinations together with the unreranked baseline of each system.
\input{tables/reranking.tex}

Three patterns emerge, and none of them is the one usually expected of a reranking cascade.

First, \textbf{reranking does not improve the systems that are already strong}. BM25-unigram falls from $0.869$ to $0.766$ under the best blend configuration, and the hybrid from $0.893$ to $0.706$. The only system that benefits is the weakest, BGE-M3-ColBERT, which rises from $0.448$ to $0.475$. A cross-encoder trained on topical relevance can repair a ranking that has the right family in the wrong order; it cannot improve one that is already discriminating variants correctly, because it does not represent the distinction being made.

Second, \textbf{blend always beats replace, by a wide margin}: $+0.33$ to $+0.39$ in Acc@1. Ranking by cross-encoder score alone discards precisely the lexical evidence that resolves parametric variants. The gap is a direct measure of how much of the useful signal lives in the first stage.

Third, \textbf{depth barely matters, and what effect it has runs in opposite directions for the two strategies}. Under replace, accuracy degrades monotonically as $K'$ grows --- BM25 falls from $0.451$ at $K'=20$ to $0.427$ at $K'=100$, the hybrid from $0.451$ to $0.426$ --- because each additional candidate is another opportunity for the cross-encoder to promote a topically plausible but parametrically wrong item. Under blend the three depths are within $0.004$ of each other for every system, with a slight preference for the deepest. The declared choice of depth, in other words, was never the decision it appeared to be: what determines the outcome is whether the first-stage score is kept, not how many candidates are rescored.

The practical reading is that a general-purpose reranker has nothing to add here. Its training objective --- is this passage about this question --- is satisfied equally by every variant of a template, which is precisely the distinction the task requires. Reranking would only help if the reranker were trained to compare parameter values, and no off-the-shelf model is.

\subsection{Query Expansion with Generated Documents}
\label{subsec:hyde_results}

Table~\ref{tab:hyde} reports HyDE on both families. The prediction was that it would hurt. It does, by more than expected, and the two retrievers fail in different ways --- which is what makes the result worth reporting.

\input{tables/hyde.tex}

\paragraph{The generated document does not contain the parameters.}
Applying the rule-based extractor of Section~\ref{subsec:structured} to the hypothetical documents instead of to the queries measures directly what survives generation. From the query it recovers $97.3\%$ of axes correctly and a complete tuple for $87.5\%$ of queries. From the hypothetical document it recovers $35.9\%$ of axes, leaves $61.3\%$ unmentioned, and produces a complete tuple for \emph{none} of the $1{,}976$ queries. The model reliably paraphrases the opening of the query --- concept, material, diameter --- and reliably drops the parenthetical block that carries work shift, maintenance window and execution volume. It writes a plausible specification for the family and discards the variant.

\paragraph{For the dense retriever, that is exactly the dissociation again.}
BGE-M3-ColBERT keeps $0.963$ at parent level with the hypothetical document alone, barely below its $0.988$ on the real query, while item-level accuracy falls from $0.493$ to $0.085$. HyDE preserves precisely what dense retrieval on this catalog already does well and destroys precisely what it already does badly. As an intervention it is not neutral-to-harmful; it is aimed at the wrong half of the problem.

\paragraph{For BM25 the failure is of a different kind, and it is instructive.}
BM25 loses the family as well: parent accuracy falls from $0.955$ to $0.092$. The cause is not dilution but inverse document frequency, and the numbers are worth spelling out because they invert the usual intuition. This catalog's vocabulary is $337$ word types, and its content words are in almost every record: \textit{canalización} occurs in $46{,}117$ of the $47{,}513$ items and therefore carries an idf of $0.03$; \textit{tubos} occurs in $45{,}739$ and carries $0.04$. Meanwhile the Spanish clitic \textit{se} occurs in exactly one item, and carries the maximum idf in the index, $10.36$. Generated prose is full of such words. Summed over its terms the average query carries $17.9$ of idf mass; the average hypothetical document carries $56.1$, adding $14.4$ in-vocabulary terms the query did not have, and \textit{se} alone --- present in $76\%$ of the generated documents --- outweighs half of the original query on its own. BM25 then ranks by which documents happen to contain those words, which is close to arbitrary with respect to what was asked.

This is worth stating as a general caution rather than a curiosity. Generated text is fluent, and fluency in a collection of terse technical records is anomalous. A retrieval model that weights terms by their rarity in the collection will read fluency as evidence. Concatenating the query with the hypothetical document does not repair it for BM25 ($0.048$), and only partly for ColBERT ($0.331$ against $0.493$), because the added mass does not go away by adding the original query back.

\subsection{Structured Attribute Matching}
\label{subsec:structured_results}

If the catalog is generated from templates and attribute tuples, the obvious objection to everything above is that retrieval is the wrong tool: extract the attributes and look the item up. Table~\ref{tab:structured_baselines} reports that alternative, built as described in Section~\ref{subsec:structured} and measured on the same queries as every other system in this study.

\input{tables/structured_baselines.tex}

The answer to the objection is that structured matching is competitive but does not win. The best configuration --- rule-based extraction --- reaches $0.903$ at item level, above every neural system and above the best hybrid ($0.893$), and still $7.0$ points below BM25 with parameter tokens ($0.974$; paired bootstrap $95\%$ CI $[6.6, 7.5]$ points, $p < 0.0001$). Replacing stage 1 with the ground-truth template adds only $1.0$ point, to $0.914$: even told which template the query belongs to, structured matching does not close the gap. Why it does not is the subject of the last part of this section, and the answer is not the one the framing of the objection suggests.

\paragraph{The pipeline is the dissociation, made mechanical.}
This family separates the two levels of the task by construction: stage 1 decides the family, stages 2 and 3 decide the variant. The measurements follow exactly. All five configurations share stage 1, and all five score \emph{identically} at parent level, $0.9848$; the three variants given the ground-truth template score $1.0000$ by construction. At item level the same five span $0.031$ to $0.903$. Nothing about the family-level task distinguishes these systems, and everything about the variant-level task does.

The comparison with the dense retriever they all contain makes the point sharper. Multilingual-e5-base alone scores $0.982$ at parent level and $0.136$ at item level. Attaching an exact lookup over extracted attributes to that same retriever leaves the parent level where it was and moves the item level to $0.903$. The dense encoder was never the part that was failing to find the family.

The residual difference between those two parent-level figures --- $0.9848$ against $0.9817$ --- is the one respect in which this family is not run exactly like the rest of the study: the structured pipelines feed stage 1 the normalised query text, while the dense index they call was built on the raw form. Re-running the rule-based pipeline on raw text reproduces $0.9817$ exactly and moves item-level accuracy from $0.9033$ to $0.9004$, and under the ground-truth template the two runs are identical to four decimals. The choice is therefore worth $0.3$ points, all of it in stage 1, and is reported rather than absorbed.

\paragraph{Where the variants differ is stage 2, and how they fail differs more than how often.}
Table~\ref{tab:stage2} decomposes it. The rule-based extractor resolves $98.0\%$ of axes correctly, leaves $1.4\%$ unresolved and is outright wrong on $0.5\%$. The fine-tuned classifier is never uncertain --- it abstains on no axis at all --- and is wrong on $23.9\%$. The BIO tagger is almost never wrong ($0.4\%$) but leaves $54.1\%$ of axes unresolved.

\input{tables/stage2.tex}

Those two failure modes have opposite consequences, because stage 3 is a filter and not a scorer. An unresolved axis is a wildcard: the target stays in the candidate set, which merely grows. A wrong value removes the target from the set, and no reranking depth recovers it. The table of results shows this directly: the classifier's Recall@10 ($0.213$) is indistinguishable from its Acc@1 ($0.209$), while the BIO tagger's Recall@10 ($0.258$) is eight times its Acc@1 ($0.031$). One system fails by committing, the other by declining to.

\paragraph{Supervised extraction does not survive the change of text.}
The two fine-tuned extractors are trained on the long-form item description and applied to the short form used as query. Their own training logs report $0.996$ query-level accuracy and $0.974$ span $F_1$ on held-out data from the long form. Applied to the query text, the classifier produces a fully correct tuple for $22.4\%$ of queries. The drop is not a defect of the models but the result of the test they were set: the two texts describe the same item, and Section~\ref{subsec:overlap} shows the long form contains $94.1\%$ of the short form's tokens, yet a model that reads parameter values reliably in one does not read them in the other. The rule-based matcher, which learns nothing and only looks for the admissible values as literal strings, transfers without loss.

\paragraph{A filter is not a ranking.}
One further cost is visible only by comparing columns. For the rule-based pipeline the candidate set contains the target for $96.7\%$ of queries, but Acc@1 is $0.903$. The missing $6.4$ points are queries where the target is in the set and not at the top: with several items consistent with a partially resolved tuple, the pipeline has no basis for ordering them and falls back on catalog order. A retrieval model would use the rest of the query text to break that tie; this pipeline has thrown that text away by stage 3. It is the mirror image of the neural failure documented above --- a system with perfect family-level behaviour and no way to discriminate inside the family --- arrived at from the opposite direction.

\paragraph{The two approaches are the same system up to the last step.}
The comparison above is easy to read as textual retrieval beating structured matching. It is not, and the difference matters. The best lexical configuration in this study, BM25 with parameter tokens, indexes each item as its text plus one token per parameter axis (Section~\ref{subsec:lexical_methods}), and applies the same representation to the query. That representation \emph{is} a parameter tuple. Both systems therefore work from the same structured information; they differ only in what they do with it. The structured pipeline uses the tuple as an exact filter over the template's items. BM25 adds it to the query as ordinary terms and lets the ranking function weigh it against the text.

Table~\ref{tab:param_tokens} isolates the two variables --- how much the tuple contributes, and whether it matters where the tuple comes from --- by holding the index fixed and varying only how the query is formed.

\input{tables/param_tokens.tex}

The tuple is worth $10.3$ points: without it the same index and the same query text reach $0.871$, which is where every other lexical configuration in this study also sits. It is not worth anything on its own, either: querying with the tuple and no text gives $0.219$, because the axis vocabulary is shared across templates and the same tuple describes items in several of them. The two signals are complementary --- the text locates the template, the tuple locates the variant within it --- which is the item-to-parent dissociation of Section~\ref{subsec:cross_family} appearing inside a single system.

Where the tuple comes from turns out not to matter. Replacing the record's own values with values extracted from the query text --- template proposed by multilingual-e5-base, values assigned by the rule-based extractor, exactly the stages 1 and 2 of Section~\ref{subsec:structured} --- gives $0.9744$ against $0.9737$. The two agree for $93.5\%$ of queries and the disagreements cancel in aggregate. This is the control that makes the next comparison clean: it removes the source of the tuple as a variable, so what remains between the two systems is only what each does with it. It also says something about the benchmark, since a rule-based extractor is sufficient here only because the parameter values appear almost verbatim in the query text (Section~\ref{subsec:overlap}).

Given the same tuple, extracted the same way, using it as query evidence reaches $0.974$ and using it as an exact filter reaches $0.903$. The seven-point difference is the cost of the filter's rigidity, and Table~\ref{tab:stage2} has already shown its mechanism: the extractor is wrong on $0.5\%$ of axes and unsure on $1.4\%$, and under an exact filter each of those either removes the target from the candidate set or leaves it in an unordered blob, while under BM25 a wrong parameter token merely subtracts its idf from one document's score and the text decides the rest. The recommendation that follows is not ``use lexical retrieval instead of structured matching'' but the narrower and more useful one: extract the attributes, and then rank with them rather than filter on them.

\paragraph{What this costs to build.}
Both approaches need the same two artefacts: a machine-readable schema for every template, and something that assigns values to axes. They exist here because the catalog is generated from that schema. What separates them is robustness to their own errors, not the effort of construction --- which is why the failure of the two supervised extractors is a cost borne by both, and why the softer combination absorbs it better.

\subsubsection{Method-Specific Error Patterns}

One limit is imposed by the representation before any scoring happens. After normalisation the whole catalog uses only $337$ distinct word types, and $47{,}513$ items are built from them. Under BM25-unigram's weighting, $3{,}276$ documents in $1{,}602$ groups end up with \emph{identical} sparse vectors: for those items the index cannot tell one from another at all. They cover $6.94\%$ of the test queries, which caps BM25-unigram's attainable Acc@1 at $0.9645$ --- against a measured $0.8691$, so collisions account for about a third of the headroom it leaves.

Adding the $61$ parameter tokens raises the vocabulary to $398$ types and removes every collision: the parameter-token index has no duplicate vectors at all. Part of the $10.3$ points those tokens are worth is therefore not better weighting but a representation that can distinguish the items in the first place. Injectivity alone does not explain the gain, though: the uni+bigram index has $1{,}142$ types and no duplicates either, and scores $0.8267$, below plain unigram. What the parameter tokens add is not vocabulary but the specific $61$ distinctions the catalog is organised around.

The hybrid has the lowest right-parent-wrong-item rate of the three ($8.13\%$ against $10.38\%$ for BM25 and $54.86\%$ for ColBERT), which is where its advantage over BM25 comes from: the character n-gram and neural signals resolve a subset of the within-template confusions that BM25 alone gets wrong. Its wrong-parent rate, however, is essentially BM25's ($2.60\%$ against $2.71\%$), so the fusion helps inside the template and not outside it.

\subsection{Overall Performance Summary}
\label{sec:4.7_Overall Performance Summary}

Figure~\ref{fig:acc1_by_family} consolidates the central finding visually: the best configuration of each method family is plotted at both evaluation levels. Lexical retrieval (BM25-unigram-params) leads at item level by a wide margin, while neural retrieval (BGE-M3-ColBERT) collapses on the same task --- yet recovers to near-perfect performance at parent level. Seen across all six families at once, the result is sharper than the neural-versus-lexical comparison suggests. At parent level the six span a range of just $2.3$ percentage points, from $0.974$ to $0.997$: every approach tested, from term weighting to late interaction to rule-based attribute matching, identifies the parametric family essentially perfectly. At item level the same six span $52.6$ points. \emph{Locating the family is not a discriminating task in this catalog; choosing the variant is the whole problem.} The hybrid and reranking families are instructive here because they mix both kinds of signal: reranking reaches the second-highest parent-level score of the study ($0.992$) while dropping to $0.766$ at item level, which is the dissociation reproduced inside a single system.

The evaluation produced four findings: (1) BM25 over the catalog text plus the query's parameter tokens achieves the highest item-level accuracy ($97.4\%$); (2) the best neural retriever trails it by $52.6$ percentage points, while matching it at parent level; (3) fusing a lexical and a neural ranker roughly doubles neural-only accuracy ($89.3\%$ against $44.8\%$) without reaching the lexical baseline; and (4) neither query expansion nor cross-encoder reranking improves on that baseline --- reranking actively degrades it.

These rankings are based on Acc@1, which reflects automated deployment, where only the top result is used. It is worth checking whether a semi-automated workflow --- a human reviewer examining the first few candidates --- would reorder them, and it would not: BM25 with parameter tokens also has the highest Recall@5 of any system evaluated here ($0.985$, against $0.944$ for the best hybrid and $0.948$ for the best reranked configuration). The choice of metric does not rescue the neural and hybrid systems on this benchmark.


\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The result that organises the rest is the dissociation. On this catalog, identifying the parametric family is close to solved by anything --- nine of the ten systems in Table~\ref{tab:dissociation} place the correct family first for at least $96\%$ of queries, the exception being a monolingual sentence-similarity model that is weak at both levels --- and identifying the variant is not solved by most of them. That the same query, the same corpus and the same ranking produce $99.8\%$ and $7.0\%$ depending only on which level is scored means that a single accuracy figure on a parametric catalog says almost nothing until the level is stated. Studies that report family-level or category-level accuracy on such catalogs, as much of the prior work in this domain does, are measuring the part of the task that is already easy.

That a well-tuned lexical configuration ends up on top is consistent with prior findings that optimized lexical models surpass neural approaches in domain-specific settings with consistent terminology~\cite{thakur2021beir}, and with work showing that term dictionaries improve AEC search precision~\cite{fan2015project}. But the reading a practitioner should take from it is narrower than ``use BM25''. The configuration that reaches $97.4\%$ is BM25 plus a step that resolves the query's parameter values against the template schema; without that step the same index reaches $87.1\%$, in line with every other lexical configuration here. What is doing the work is making the parameter values explicit, and that is available to any ranker.

Neural retrievers, by contrast, excel at high-level semantic categorization but struggle with fine-grained item matching. At the parent level, models like BGE-M3-ColBERT achieved approximately 99.7\% accuracy, rivaling lexical methods; however, at the item level, their performance lagged due to vocabulary mismatch and lack of domain adaptation. General-purpose embeddings underperform on specialized tasks because they handle rare terms poorly ~\cite{tang2024we}, and dense retrievers generalize poorly without fine-tuning ~\cite{li2022domain}. Construction catalogs combine technical jargon, codes, and measurements infrequent in general corpora—terms BM25 handles well via inverse document frequency. One aspect of the comparison should be stated plainly, because it is a design choice and not an oversight. The best lexical configuration consumes the parameter values that the BC3 format declares for every item, on both the document and the query side; the neural models were run zero-shot on text alone. Nothing prevents the same tokens from being given to a dense retriever, and the obvious next experiment is to do exactly that. What the present comparison establishes is narrower and still useful: a term-weighting model that is handed the parametric structure resolves the variant, and a sentence encoder that is handed the same prose does not.

Hybrid methods improved performance but did not outperform standalone BM25, diverging from general-domain findings where convex combination fusion often outperforms reciprocal rank fusion with minimal training data ~\cite{bruch2023analysis}. In our setting, BM25's near-optimal accuracy left little headroom for neural signals to contribute meaningfully, suggesting that hybridization may introduce noise in highly technical domains. Learned sparse methods like SPLADE offer a compromise by combining semantic understanding with sparse term expansions ~\cite{formal2021splade}; our BGE-sparse variant rivaled ColBERT at parent level, though SPLADE's complexity remains a deployment barrier.

Cross-encoder reranking did not help any system that was already strong. Ranking by cross-encoder score alone degraded BM25 from $0.869$ to $0.434$, and blending it with the first-stage score ($\lambda=0.6$) recovered most but not all of the loss, to $0.766$. The hybrid behaved the same way, falling from $0.893$ to $0.706$ under the best blend. Only BGE-M3-ColBERT, the weakest of the three, improved at all ($0.448 \to 0.475$). A reranker trained on topical relevance can reorder a list that has the right family in the wrong order; it has no representation of the distinction between one parametric variant and another, so it cannot improve a ranking that is already making that distinction correctly~\cite{nogueiraDocumentRankingPretrained2020, zhuang2023rankt5}.

The error analysis constrains the available explanations more than it might appear. Subword tokenization is the standard account of why neural encoders mishandle numbers: it breaks a value into fragments and impairs magnitude representation~\cite{wallace2019nlp}. That account cannot be the whole story here, because the failure profile is shared. Numeric mismatches make up $98.2\%$ of BGE-M3-ColBERT's errors, $97.5\%$ of the hybrid's --- and $98.0\%$ of BM25's, which has no subword tokenizer and no notion of magnitude at all. What the three systems have in common is not an encoder but a task: distinguishing documents whose only difference is a number, in a catalog where the whole vocabulary is $337$ word types. The residual errors are the same errors regardless of representation, and the systems differ in how often they make them, not in what they are.

Construction documents combine structured codes with technical free text, and NLP tools tailored to construction achieve high precision by leveraging both cues~\cite{AKANBI2021}, while specifications encode procedural logic that generic retrievers ignore~\cite{LI2023}. Our results point the same way from a different direction: what closed the gap here was not a better encoder but making the structured half of the document explicit to the ranker.

Prior work focused on classification using rule-based and ML approaches ~\cite{caldasAutomatingHierarchicalDocument2003, natural_wu_2022, martinez-rojasApproachAutomaticClassification2015, role_martnezrojas_2016, martinez-rojasUsingClassificationTechniques2018}, but these studies use small datasets and do not benchmark retrieval across method families. Recent work has noted persistent challenges with construction-specific jargon ~\cite{natural_wu_2022} and domain adaptation for language models ~\cite{chungComparingNaturalLanguage2023}. Our study fills this gap by systematically evaluating retrieval on a large-scale catalog, where fine-grained matching reveals effectiveness differences invisible at the category level.

For industry applications, domain-tuned lexical retrieval should be the default. We recommend implementing optimized BM25 with domain-aware preprocessing, conducting targeted error analysis, and exploring neural reranking only when limitations stem from semantic gaps rather than terminology mismatches. Improving data quality often delivers greater returns than model complexity; if neural approaches are pursued, pseudo-label fine-tuning offers a practical path ~\cite{li2022domain}.

Information retrieval benchmarks must also account for latency and hardware constraints ~\cite{santhanam-etal-2023-moving}. BM25 offers sub-100ms latency on standard hardware, while neural retrievers require GPUs with 2–10× overhead. Lexical methods accommodate catalog updates without retraining, and interpretability favors BM25's transparent term matching over opaque neural scores --- though learned sparse retrievers offer a middle ground ~\cite{bruch2023analysis}. Retrieval capabilities should  be integrated in tools like BIM software to support semi-automated 5D workflows.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results, and one of them is fundamental.

\paragraph{Queries are not written by practitioners} The queries are the catalog's own \textit{resumen} field, paired with the \textit{texto} field of the same record, so the target contains $94.1\%$ of the query's tokens and $99.96\%$ of its numeric values verbatim. This is the control described in Section~\ref{sec:introduction} and it bounds what the results mean: they establish how these methods rank when the query is already expressed in catalog vocabulary, and say nothing about how they rank when it is not. A system that discriminates variants well here might still fail on a site engineer's phrasing, and one that fails here will not be rescued by better phrasing. Extending the benchmark with queries collected from estimators would let the two capabilities be measured against each other rather than one at a time, and is the most useful thing that could be added to this work.

\paragraph{A single, homogeneous subcategory} The dataset covers one subcategory of the ADIF catalog, the chapter dedicated to platform conduit systems (`OEB'): $47{,}513$ items generated from $30$ templates, all following the same structural pattern and drawing on the same attribute vocabulary. This homogeneity is what makes the benchmark a demanding test of variant discrimination, and it is also what limits generalisation: chapters with greater lexical diversity, such as track infrastructure or electrification, would place a different balance of demands on lexical and semantic matching. Whether the item-to-parent dissociation persists, narrows or widens across such chapters is an open question.
Additional limitations include the zero-shot evaluation of neural models. While this setup reflects realistic deployment constraints where annotated domain-specific data is unavailable, it leaves open the question of how much domain-adapted fine-tuning could narrow the performance gap with lexical methods. The cross-encoder employed (mmarco-mMiniLMv2-L12-H384-v1) was trained on mMARCO, which includes Spanish, so its underperformance cannot be attributed to a cross-lingual gap. A more plausible explanation is that its notion of relevance is topical: mMARCO pairs a question with a passage that answers it, a criterion under which every variant of a parametric template is equally relevant to a query naming that template. The reranker is therefore being asked to make a distinction it was never trained to make, which is consistent with the numeric mismatches that dominate its errors. Finally, SPLADE was excluded due to limited Spanish language support, and the single-correct-item assumption may oversimplify practical deployment scenarios where multiple catalog entries represent acceptable substitutes.



\section{Conclusions and Future Work}

We evaluated lexical, neural, hybrid and structured retrieval on $47{,}513$ items of a Spanish railway catalog, $47{,}508$ of them generated from $25$ parametric templates, scoring every system both on the exact variant and on its parent family.

The central finding is a dissociation between those two levels. Every zero-shot multilingual dense retriever we tested identifies the correct parametric family almost perfectly --- between $96.0\%$ and $99.8\%$ --- while identifying the correct variant between $7.0\%$ and $13.6\%$ of the time. The gap reaches $92.9$ percentage points. These models have clearly learned enough about the domain to place a query in the right region of the catalog; what they do not preserve is the parameter values that separate one variant from its siblings, and those values are precisely what determines the unit price. A tuned BM25 whose queries carry the item's parameter values as explicit tokens shows almost no such gap ($97.4\%$ against $98.5\%$), and an exact structured lookup over the same extracted values reaches $90.3\%$. The comparison between those two is the second finding of this work, and it is not the one the retrieval-versus-structured framing predicts: both need the same parameter tuple, and extracting it from the query text rather than reading it from the catalog costs nothing measurable ($0.9744$ against $0.9737$). What separates them is only what they do with it. Used as query evidence the tuple yields $97.4\%$; used as an exact filter, $90.3\%$. The seven points are the price of a hard constraint in a pipeline whose extractor is wrong about one axis in two hundred.

Two things bound these conclusions, both of them by design. The queries are the catalog's own short descriptions, which removes the vocabulary gap in order to isolate the parametric one; results here transfer to catalog-to-catalog matching and say nothing about a practitioner's phrasing. And the lexical systems consume the parameter values the BC3 format declares, while the neural ones were run zero-shot on text alone; giving a dense retriever the same structured input is the experiment this study sets up rather than performs. Within those bounds the dissociation is robust: it holds across single-vector, learned-sparse and late-interaction architectures, it holds in attenuated form in the lexical family too, and it is the property to test for before deploying any retriever over a parametric catalog.


Four recommendations follow directly from what was measured here, and one thing that is often recommended does not. Report accuracy at the level the application needs: on a parametric catalog, a family-level figure can be near-ceiling while the variant-level figure that determines the unit price is in single digits. Make the attribute values explicit to the ranker rather than hoping the encoder preserves them --- appending resolved parameter tokens to text was worth $10.3$ points here, more than any change of model. Extract those attributes, but rank with them instead of filtering on them: the same tuple used as a hard constraint costs seven points, because an extractor that is wrong once in two hundred axes removes the target from the candidate set altogether. And check for representational collisions before blaming the ranking function: $6.9\%$ of the queries in this benchmark have a target that some indexes cannot distinguish from another document at all.

What we cannot recommend, on this evidence, is a general-purpose cross-encoder as a precision stage. It degraded every system here that was already strong, and helped only the weakest.

Several research directions warrant further investigation. Cross-domain validation across other ADIF catalog chapters and external taxonomies would strengthen generalizability ~\cite{royanoAnalysisClassificationSystems2023a}, while multilingual expansion could address terminology drift and locale-specific conventions ~\cite{valentini2024messirve,tang2024we,jungyeon2024cross-lingual}. Deployment studies examining informal price-driven queries, efficiency–effectiveness trade-offs ~\cite{bruch2023analysis}, and integration with BIM and estimation tools remain necessary, and field validation with construction professionals is essential to assess whether ranking improvements yield measurable productivity gains.

Technical gaps also persist. Neural encoders need specialized tokenization for numbers, tolerances, and unit conversions. Structured vocabularies and ontologies remain underused despite their utility for disambiguation ~\cite{yan2022overview,schlachter2021linked}. Catalog-scale search should optimize both quality and cost/latency ~\cite{bruch2023analysis}, and adversarial probes can expose brittleness and prevent silent failures in safety-critical selections ~\cite{wallace2019nlp}.

Finally, well-measured retrieval is foundational for automation in estimating, compliance checking, and procurement. Our benchmark can accelerate reproducible research, while deployment guidance aims to reduce costly mismatches. However, indiscriminate automation may amplify biases or degrade oversight; we recommend phased rollouts with human-in-the-loop review for high-stakes selections.

  \item \textbf{The exact query set}, as a versioned list of catalog identifiers with a checksum, rather than a sampling procedure to be re-run. Every system reported here was evaluated on that same list, and the check that enforces it is part of the released code.

  \item The evaluation code, including the dual item/parent scorer, and the YAML configuration of every run.
\end{itemize}

Releasing per-query outputs rather than only aggregate scores is what allows the two levels of evaluation to be recomputed, error taxonomies to be redefined, and the results to be audited against a different metric or a different notion of relevance.

\section{Technical Background on Retrieval Methods}
\label{app:technical-background}

\subsection{Information Retrieval Methods}

\subsubsection{Lexical Methods}

Traditional information retrieval relies on lexical matching between queries and documents through term-based scoring functions. TF-IDF (Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency) weighs terms by their frequency within documents and rarity across the collection ~\cite{salton1988term, manning2008introduction}.

BM25 extends TF-IDF with sophisticated length normalization and term frequency saturation ~\cite{robertson2009probabilistic}:
\begin{equation}
\text{BM25}(q,d) = \sum_{t \in q} \text{IDF}(t) \cdot \frac{f(t,d) \cdot (k_1 + 1)}{f(t,d) + k_1 \cdot (1 - b + b \cdot \frac{|d|}{\text{avgdl}})}
\end{equation}
where $f(t,d)$ is the frequency of term $t$ in document $d$, $|d|$ is the document length, $\text{avgdl}$ is the average document length in the collection, $k_1$ controls term frequency saturation (typically 0.9--2.0), and $b$ controls length normalization (typically 0.4--0.8). Recent studies demonstrate that properly tuned BM25 can outperform neural models in specialized domains, particularly when queries and documents share consistent terminology ~\cite{thakur2021beir}. The importance of parameter tuning for BM25 cannot be overstated --- performance can vary significantly across different $k_1$ and $b$ values depending on collection characteristics ~\cite{trotmanImprovementsBM25Language2014}.

Character n-gram variants of TF-IDF provide robustness to spelling variations and partial matches, particularly valuable in technical domains with abbreviations and compound terms ~\cite{mcnameeCharacterNGramTokenization2004}. These methods generate overlapping character sequences (e.g., 3--5 characters), enabling matching even when exact terms differ slightly.

\subsubsection{Pseudo-Relevance Feedback}

Pseudo-relevance feedback (PRF) methods enhance retrieval by assuming top-ranked documents from an initial search are relevant and using them to reformulate queries ~\cite{rocchio1971relevance}.

Relevance Model 3 (RM3) interpolates the original query with a relevance model estimated from top-ranked documents ~\cite{abdul2004umass}:
\begin{equation}
P(t|q') = \lambda P(t|q) + (1-\lambda) \sum_{d \in R} P(t|d) P(d|q)
\end{equation}
where $q'$ is the expanded query, $t$ is a candidate term, $P(t|q)$ is the term probability in the original query, $P(t|d)$ is the term probability in document $d$, $P(d|q)$ is the document relevance score, $R$ is the set of top-ranked pseudo-relevant documents, and $\lambda$ balances original and expansion terms. RM3 consistently improves retrieval effectiveness but requires careful tuning of the number of feedback documents and terms ~\cite{lv2010empirical}.

Rocchio's method adjusts query vectors based on centroids of relevant and non-relevant documents ~\cite{rocchio1971relevance}:
\begin{equation}
\vec{q'} = \alpha \vec{q} + \beta \frac{1}{|R|} \sum_{d \in R} \vec{d} - \gamma \frac{1}{|N|} \sum_{d \in N} \vec{d}
\end{equation}
where $\vec{q}$ and $\vec{q'}$ are the original and modified query vectors, $\vec{d}$ represents document vectors, $R$ and $N$ are the sets of relevant and non-relevant documents respectively, and $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$ control the influence of each component. While originally designed for vector space models, Rocchio adapts well to modern retrieval systems ~\cite{cao2008selecting}.

\subsubsection{Neural Retrieval Methods}

Modern neural approaches transform the retrieval problem into dense vector similarity search. Bi-encoder architectures independently encode queries and documents into fixed-dimensional representations, enabling efficient similarity computation through inner products or cosine similarity ~\cite{karpukhin2020dense}. The models evaluated in this study --- E5, GTE and BGE-M3 --- are described in Section~\ref{subsec:neural_methods}.

Sparse neural methods like SPLADE learn term importance weights while maintaining the interpretability of lexical matching ~\cite{formal2021splade}. Late-interaction models balance effectiveness and efficiency through token-level interactions. ColBERT (Contextualized Late Interaction over BERT) encodes queries and documents into bags of contextualized embeddings, computing relevance through the sum of maximum similarities ~\cite{khattab2020colbert}:
\begin{equation}
\text{Score}(q,d) = \sum_{t_q \in q} \max_{t_d \in d} \text{sim}(E_q(t_q), E_d(t_d))
\end{equation}

Recent multi-representation models, such as BGE-M3, combine dense, sparse, and multi-vector strategies within a single framework, adapting to different retrieval scenarios ~\cite{chen2024bge}. These unified models show particular promise for multilingual and domain-specific applications where different representation strategies may excel for different query types.

\subsection{Hybrid and Reranking Approaches}

\subsubsection{Fusion and Reranking}

Combining multiple retrieval signals often improves performance by leveraging complementary strengths. This study uses weighted linear combination of min-max-normalised scores, defined in Section~\ref{subsec:hybrid_fusion}; rank-level alternatives such as Reciprocal Rank Fusion avoid score calibration altogether ~\cite{cormack2009reciprocal, lin2021pretrained}, and learned combination weights are also possible ~\cite{macdonald2013learning}.

Cross-encoders differ from bi-encoders in encoding the query and the document jointly, so that cross-attention can match their terms directly ~\cite{nogueira2020passage}. This is more effective and far more expensive, which is why they are applied only to rerank the top $K'$ candidates of a cheaper first stage. The literature reports consistent gains across domains ~\cite{zhuang2023rankt5}; Section~\ref{subsec:reranking} reports that this benchmark is an exception.

\subsection{Evaluation in Technical Domains}

\subsubsection{Metrics for Precision-Critical Tasks}

Evaluation in technical retrieval requires metrics sensitive to exact matching and early precision. Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) measures the inverse rank of the first relevant document, particularly suitable for known-item search ~\cite{craswell2009mean}:
\begin{equation}
\text{MRR} = \frac{1}{|Q|} \sum_{q \in Q} \frac{1}{\text{rank}_q}
\end{equation}
where $Q$ is the set of queries and $\text{rank}_q$ is the position of the first relevant document for query $q$.

Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (nDCG) accounts for graded relevance and position bias ~\cite{jarvelin2002cumulated}:
\begin{equation}
\text{nDCG@k} = \frac{\text{DCG@k}}{\text{IDCG@k}}, \quad \text{DCG@k} = \sum_{i=1}^k \frac{2^{\text{rel}_i} - 1}{\log_2(i+1)}
\end{equation}
where $k$ is the cutoff rank, $\text{rel}_i$ is the relevance score of the document at position $i$, and $\text{IDCG@k}$ is the ideal DCG obtained from a perfect ranking.

For technical domains where users expect exact matches, Precision@1 (Success@1) becomes critical, while Recall@k measures the system's ability to retrieve relevant items within reasonable examination depth ~\cite{baeza-yates_riberio-neto_1999}.

\subsubsection{Domain-Specific Evaluation Challenges}

Technical domains present unique evaluation challenges that standard benchmarks may not address. Near-duplicate documents --- common in parametric catalogs --- complicate relevance judgments, as multiple items may satisfy an information need ~\cite{bernstein2005redundant}. Some evaluation frameworks credit any item from the same parametric family (parent-level evaluation), while others require exact matches (item-level evaluation) ~\cite{piroi2011clef}.

Numeric parameters in technical documents require special consideration. Queries mentioning specific dimensions or quantities should retrieve documents with matching values, yet standard text similarity may not capture numeric equivalence ~\cite{sarawagi2004efficient}. Prior work in patent retrieval ~\cite{piroi2011clef}, biomedical search ~\cite{simpson2014}, and legal document retrieval ~\cite{locke2017automatic} demonstrates the importance of domain-adapted evaluation protocols that account for these characteristics.
